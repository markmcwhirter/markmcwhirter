Culture, Study, Exegesis September 2, 2026

Culture Matters

Pick any important topic on modern Christian life, and the Word of God will have something to say about it. You will also see there are significant differences in how many movements and denominations interpret the scriptures.  When delving deeply into scripture an analysis or exegesis is required to arrive at the meaning and the guidance required. There are common ways to examine the Word,  including a comparison of historical, cultural and modern cultural views. This is vitally important in determining the context in which the scriptures were written.  Most all the new testament was written to churches to address certain current issues that they faced, and had specific remedies for the situation.  

The main idea is to carefully consider the cultural context when studying scripture.  It is only by including the examination of scripture through that lens can you arrive at a true interpretation that can be applied to everyday modern life.

I’d like to first discuss traditions or situations that we rarely see in the modern Western church.

Paul tells the Romans to greet one another with a holy kiss. He tells the Corinthians the same thing in two different letters. Peter says it too, calling it a kiss of love. Four apostolic commands, plain imperatives, no qualifiers, no hint that anyone should treat this as optional.

I have rarely seen anyone greeted with a holy kiss at church. Probably neither have you. This is also true in traditions that stress reading Scripture at face value. Paul tells Timothy to stop drinking only water and to take a little wine for his stomach. Readers treat this as advice for one man with digestive trouble, not as a command for everyone. Jesus washes feet and says his followers should wash one another's feet. A few traditions keep this practice, while most read it as a lesson about service. Paul also requires women to cover their heads when they pray or prophesy. He supports this rule by appealing to creation, angels, nature, and the practice of every church he knows. Yet most churches that claim to follow the text strictly have not used head coverings for a century.

At the same time, these readers treat love your enemies, do not commit adultery, and husbands, love your wives as commands that still bind Christians. They are right to do so.

So here is the thing that ought to be obvious and somehow isn't. Nobody reads the New Testament flat — nobody lets every command in it press with the same weight, so that the kiss obliges you exactly the way the adultery prohibition does. The page has contours on it before anyone starts reading. Not one person, not one church, not one tradition. Every reader already sorts commands into the ones that transfer and the ones that don't, and the sorting happens whether or not anyone has a theory for it. The only real question is whether you do that sorting with a method you can state out loud and defend, or whether you do it by inherited reflex and then justify after the fact when somebody presses you.

That's what this essay is about. And my argument is that a serious method exists, that it's mostly native to the Bible rather than introduced from outside the Word, and that running it honestly on contentious issues produces different kinds of answers. Not one answer applied different times, but different results, because the evidence is genuinely different in each case.

Two escape hatches, both closed shut

Before the method, the two ways people try to avoid needing one.

"Just read it literally." -  This will obviously fail.

It fails because nobody does it. The holy kiss, the foot basin, the head covering, the braided hair and gold that both Paul and Peter prohibit, the wine for the stomach, the blood and things strangled that Acts 15 actually decreed — the traditions most committed to flat reading apply it selectively. The selectivity might be correct. It's still selectivity, and selectivity needs an argument.

It fails because the New Testament doesn't do it. I'll get to Acts 15 in a minute, but consider Paul taking a farming rule about not muzzling an ox and applying it to ministerial pay, then asking rhetorically whether God is really concerned about oxen. That is a lot of things. Flat is not one of them.

It fails because most of the New Testament is occasional literature — letters to specific congregations about specific crises — and the authors keep telling you so. Now concerning the matters about which you wrote. Because of the present distress. I say this as a concession, not a command. I am saying this for your own benefit, not to lay any restraint upon you. Treating occasional mail as a decontextualized legal code isn't fidelity to the text. It's a genre error, which is a species of misreading.

And it fails because anyone reading an English Bible is already downstream of thousands of interpretive decisions about culturally loaded vocabulary. Doulos as servant or slave. Kephalē as head, in which of its several Greek senses. Authentein as have authority or as domineer. Arsenokoitai as any of a dozen renderings that carry wildly different implications. There is no unmediated plain text sitting underneath the translation waiting to be obeyed. The choice is between examined cultural judgment and unexamined cultural judgment, and unexamined is not the same thing as absent.

"It's all just culture." This fails harder, and it's the more fashionable failure.

If the criteria for setting a text aside are loose enough, they'll set aside anything the reader finds inconvenient, and the text stops functioning as an authority at all. It becomes a mirror with a nice binding.

What separates a disciplined cultural analysis from a solvent is testable. A real method has to be able to produce results the interpreter dislikes — if every application lands exactly on the conclusions you already held, the criteria are decoration. It has to apply the same tests to texts you want to keep. If culturally conditioned gets deployed against the gender passages but never against the ones on greed, the asymmetry is the argument's actual content and everything else is stage dressing. It has to say in advance what would count as evidence against it. And it has to accept that the burden of proof sits on whoever claims an apostolic command doesn't transfer, because texts are presumed to bind until shown otherwise.

None of that is a conservative requirement or a liberal one. It's the difference between an argument and a preference.

Three different distances

One more clarification, because a lot of arguments are people talking past each other about which problem they're solving.

There's a language gap. Words don't map. Greek sarx is not English flesh. A first-century oikos is not a suburban nuclear family. Ancient friendship carried patronage obligations that are invisible to us. This gap closes with lexical and historical work, and it's a problem of understanding, not yet of application.

There's a situation gap. Some instruction addresses circumstances that are simply gone — meat sold in temple markets, the legal position of widows, patron-client obligation, Roman household law. Where the situation has vanished you can't reissue the instruction; you have to understand it as a response to a problem and then ask what responding to the analogous problem looks like.

And there's a moral gap. Some instruction regulates an institution the church has come to think is itself unjust. Slavery, above all. Here the difficulty isn't that circumstances changed but that the church's moral judgment changed, and it believes Scripture drove the change. This is the hard one, and it's the one that generates all the interesting methodology, because it forces the question of whether the text can be ahead of its own explicit rules.

Most debates about biblical guidance for modern issues turn on which gap is involved. Complementarians and egalitarians agree that language can create distance. They disagree on whether the gender passages respond to temporary situations. Traditional and affirming readers on sexuality also agree that the language gap must be addressed. They disagree on whether the modern issue creates a moral gap.

The Bible does this to itself

Now the part that convinced me none of this is a modern invention designed to get around inconvenient verses.

Jesus goes behind the law. In Matthew 19 the Pharisees ask about divorce, and Jesus does something that should be much more famous than it is. He separates what Moses permitted from what God intended, and he dates the difference. Moses allowed the certificate because of your hardness of heart. From the beginning it was not so. Then he quotes Genesis against Deuteronomy.

Sit with what that means. A divinely given regulation gets classified as accommodation to human failure rather than as the standard itself. Not every divine rule expresses the divine mind. Some law is damage control.

And notice the constraints, because they matter enormously for whether this move can be abused. Jesus argues for it in public against trained opponents on grounds they could in principle assess. He appeals to a text with better creational credentials, not to contemporary sentiment. And he applies it restrictively — the result is a tighter divorce standard, not a looser one. Anyone who wants to use the accommodation principle to loosen something should notice that its inventor used it to tighten.

The Jerusalem council doesn't transfer law flat. Acts 15 is the clearest thing in the New Testament on cultural boundary reasoning, and it's remarkable. Circumcision is an everlasting covenant in Genesis 17. Reaffirmed in Torah. Practiced by Jesus and every apostle in the room. And the council decides Gentiles don't need it.

Notice how the council reaches its decision. It considers the Spirit's visible work among uncircumcised Gentiles. It uses theology about grace and burdens, reads Amos through Christ, and makes practical judgments about shared meals and Jewish concerns. Experience, theology, interpretation, and wisdom work together. The result goes beyond what a plain reading of the Torah seemed to allow. The New Testament preserves this decision as a model.

Then the punchline. They kept four rules, one of which — abstaining from blood and things strangled — most of the church stopped observing. So the church later ran on the Acts 15 decree the exact operation Acts 15 had run on Torah.

Anyone claiming the New Testament forbids cultural transposition has to explain Acts 15. Anyone claiming transposition is unconstrained has to explain why they kept four things and argued out loud about which four.

Paul builds a category for variable practice. Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 8 through 10 establish something that gets underused in these debates: a formal class of questions where the practice itself is indifferent and what governs is conscience, love, and effect on other people. Nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean. One person esteems one day above another; another esteems all alike; let each be fully convinced in his own mind.

Here apostolic authority creates a category in which the same principle can lead different people to act differently. In that setting, forcing everyone to act the same can itself be wrong. This category matters. It shows that the New Testament allows honest differences in practice without treating them as compromise.

It also has edges. Paul does not treat porneia or idolatry or greed this way. So the category isn't a solvent, and figuring out which bucket an issue belongs in is most of the exegetical work — you don't get to settle it by assertion.

The household codes borrow a familiar form and reshape it. The Haustafeln in Colossians, Ephesians, First Peter, and the Pastoral Letters use a known Greco-Roman form for managing a household. Similar discussions appear in Aristotle's Politics, Stoic writings, and Philo. The New Testament authors then change that form.

Those changes are the important part, and both sides in modern debates appeal to them. The codes address wives, children, and slaves as responsible moral agents. Pagan parallels usually do not. In Aristotle, the male head of the house is the moral actor; everyone else is someone he manages. The New Testament also gives duties to the powerful: husbands must love their wives as Christ loved the church; fathers must not provoke their children; and masters must stop threatening and treat slaves justly. Christ frames the whole discussion: act “as to the Lord,” remember that you also have a Master in heaven, and know that he shows no partiality. In Ephesians, the code begins with “submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ.” This participle shapes what follows, although scholars debate exactly how.

People who read the codes as culturally bound point at the borrowed form and the stated apologetic purposes. People who read them as permanent point at the theological grounding, which has no pagan parallel and doesn't reduce to social prudence. Both observations are true. The fight is over which one is load-bearing.

And there's language inside the canon that outruns the canon's own rules. There is neither slave nor free. Onesimus received no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother, with Paul adding that he knows Philemon will do even more than he says, and never once writing the words set him free. Slave traders condemned in a letter that tells slaves to honor their masters. If you were called as a slave don't worry about it, but if you can gain your freedom, take it.

The key question is whether later readers may continue this movement. The other option is that these passages are pastoral adjustments within a fixed social order that readers may not extend. The entire method depends on how that question is answered.

Four ways people try to solve this

Everyone doing this work falls roughly into one of four camps, and each is right about something and blind about something else. I'll say which I think holds up.

Static application. Commands remain binding unless the text itself marks them as temporary. Such a signal might limit a command to one person, give it a short-term purpose, or tie it to an institution that no longer exists. Cultural distance may change the mode of obedience, but not the duty to obey. For example, a holy kiss becomes a warm greeting. The lasting command is to greet other believers with affection; the kiss was its first-century form.

This position has real virtues. It keeps the text able to say no to you. It has a stable, inspectable rule. It notices, correctly, that nearly every historical departure from apostolic instruction has run downhill in the direction of least social resistance, which is at minimum suggestive.

But the distinction between substance and vehicle is doing all the work and isn't itself derived from any rule. Once you've granted that greet with a holy kiss means greet warmly, you've opened a door, and nothing inside the position tells you how far it opens. And the application is conspicuously uneven. The same readers who transpose the kiss and the foot basin decline to transpose the head covering, and the difference tracks the current controversy rather than anything in the texts.

Principlizing. Find the timeless principle inside the culturally embedded command, then re-embody it now. Grasp the text in its setting, measure the river between then and now, cross the bridge, apply. Fee and Stuart ask whether a situation involves comparable particulars or whether only the underlying theology transfers. Duvall and Hays call it the Interpretive Journey. Osborne calls it a spiral to signal that you loop rather than leap.

This is what careful readers actually do, and it handles the situation gap well. It also has two problems, and the first is close to fatal.

The level-of-abstraction problem: principles can be pitched at any altitude, and the altitude determines the answer. From 1 Corinthians 14 you can extract women must be silent in church (low — transfers directly), or disruptive questioning should be handled outside the service (middle — a rule about order), or worship should be decent and orderly (high — says nothing about gender at all). Nothing in the method specifies the right altitude. Which means you can run it to any conclusion you like by adjusting one dial. Conservatives use it to smuggle cultural conservatism in as "the principle." Progressives use it to ascend until only love is left. Same dial, opposite directions.

The direction problem: once a specific command becomes a broad principle, readers must decide what that principle requires now. Their judgment, however, was shaped by the culture around them. The method therefore has no built-in defense against the same cultural pressure it is meant to resist.

Redemptive movement. The unit of meaning isn't only what a text says but which way it's pointing relative to the culture it addresses. Scripture often doesn't hand over an ultimate ethic directly; it hands over a large improvement on the surrounding norms, embedded in those norms, with markers that point past itself. So you find the movement and ask whether faithfulness means reproducing the biblical position or continuing in the biblical direction.

William Webb presents the most developed evangelical version of this approach. His book is also one of the most useful works on the subject in the past thirty years. Webb separates three points: the original culture, the biblical text, and the final ethical goal. He offers eighteen tests for deciding which commands are tied to their culture and which remain binding. Some tests carry more weight than others. The most important ask these questions: Does the instruction move toward or away from its culture's standard? Does the text contain ideas that weaken the practice it regulates? Are there exceptions where the restriction does not apply? Does the text state a purpose that could be served differently today? Does the command appeal to the fall, creation, or new creation? Did the culture offer other choices, and which one did the author select? How severe was the penalty? Does a theological parallel support the rule? Finally, does the reason for the rule depend on conditions, such as illiteracy or legal limits, that no longer exist?

Here's why I trust it more than the alternatives: it produces results its author didn't want. Webb runs the criteria and gets abolition on slavery, a substantially egalitarian result on women, and a traditional result on same-sex practice, because on that last one the criteria point the other way. He got attacked from both flanks for it. A method that fights its operator is doing something a preference can't.

The objections are serious. Grudem gives the sharpest version. First, the method places authority in a path that the interpreter projects beyond the text. The interpreter's forecast can then become the rule. Second, it can imply that the New Testament's ethics are incomplete and have been replaced. That seems to conflict with the final authority of apostolic teaching. Third, the method may have no clear stopping point. Webb answers that his tests provide real limits and can show where the path should stop. He also argues that Jesus himself uses the idea of accommodation in Matthew 19. I find that second reply stronger.

Canonical and dramatic approaches. The unit of authority is the whole story, and application means performing it rather than deducing rules from it. Wright's analogy is a Shakespeare play missing its fifth act, where a company steeped in the first four improvises an ending that is recognizably this play's ending. Vanhoozer turns doctrine into direction for performance. Hays tests proposed applications against three focal images — community, cross, new creation.

This view is right that Scripture is mainly a story, not a legal code. It is also right that faithful reading has always required judgment, not a simple lookup. Still, improvisation is hard to control. Readers choose the main images that guide the drama, and the method can become ordinary principle-based reading dressed in better prose. My deeper worry is whether it can ever tell a community that a treasured practice is wrong, instead of merely saying the practice can be staged differently.

And then there's the tradition that got it right first. Feminist, womanist, liberationist, postcolonial, and African-American readings treat the interpreter's location as evidence rather than noise. You don't have to adopt the framework to owe it something.

Two debts deserve special notice. The first is historical. In his appendix, Frederick Douglass separates the Christianity of Christ from the slaveholding religion of his country. His case is an argument about Scripture, not only a moral protest, yet many dismissed it as emotion. Lisa Bowens documents two centuries of Black readers who understood Paul as moving toward freedom, against the dominant white interpretation. They were right, but others ignored them because they seemed personally invested in the result. The second debt concerns method. As I will explain in the slavery section, Mark Noll shows that the pro-slavery side had the stronger argument under a flat reading of Scripture.

The standard objection is that foregrounding the reader's location costs you the text's power to correct the reader. The standard reply is that every reader has a location, and the readings that claim otherwise are just the ones whose location went invisible through dominance. Both are true. The second is more often forgotten.

What actually makes a command travel

Strip all of that down and you get a set of questions that genuinely discriminate. None decides anything alone. What you want is convergence.

Nearly all of them turn on the same move, and it's worth watching it work once before the lists start.

Paul tells wives to submit to their husbands. So does Titus. Near enough the same instruction — and completely different reasons bolted underneath. Ephesians hangs it on Christ and the church. Titus hangs it on that the word of God may not be reviled: behave so the neighbors have nothing to say about us.

Read only the commands and those two passages are twins. Read what each one is standing on and they aren't the same kind of thing at all. One is a claim about how the gospel is shaped. The other is a claim about the local reputation of a small and vulnerable religious minority — and a claim like that can quietly stop being true while the sentence containing it never changes a syllable.

So the question I keep asking is not what a text tells you to do. It's what the text says it's standing on. That second thing is usually the more informative half, because instructions survive the trip across nineteen centuries looking identical while the ground underneath them shifts completely.

Two limits worth admitting before the lists. Plenty of commands come with no stated reason at all, and then you're inferring one, which is a much weaker operation than reading one. And a single passage can pile up several. 1 Corinthians 11 offers four separate reasons for the head covering, which ought to make the veil harder to set aside rather than easier. Hold onto that. It turns into the sharpest problem in this essay.

Signs a command is meant to bind

It rests on God's character, or on the shape of the gospel. Be holy, for I am holy. Forgive as the Lord forgave you. You cannot make the premise culturally relative without making God culturally relative.

It rests on creation as a normative order. This one is powerful and slippery at once. Jesus uses it decisively in Matthew 19. The trouble is that Genesis 1 and 2 contain both explicit normative statements — therefore a man shall leave his father and mother, which Jesus treats as prescriptive — and bare descriptive patterns, like the order of formation, whose normative force is precisely what's in dispute. That Paul argues from creation order in 1 Timothy 2 is not in question. What that argument establishes is the entire debate.

It rests on the new creation. Instruction grounded in you have died and your life is hidden with Christ isn't culturally relative, because the premise isn't a feature of any culture.

It repeats across settings. A moral teaching has a stronger claim to remain binding when it appears in many settings: Jewish and Gentile, urban and rural, Palestine and the wider diaspora. Its case also grows when several authors use it in different kinds of writing. Such a pattern carries more weight than one command to one church during a specific crisis.

It ran against its own culture. If the instruction cut against the grain where it was written, you can't explain it as absorption. This is a strong marker, and it runs in reverse too: instruction that simply reproduces the ambient norm needs more argument to be treated as timeless. Note that this cuts in different directions on different issues, which is exactly the point of the essay.

It's an entailment of the gospel rather than a rule. Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ? The premises there aren't local.

Signs a command is bound to its moment

The text gives a purpose the practice no longer serves. This is the most useful criterion available and the most underused, and it requires nothing but reading. Titus grounds young wives' domestic submission in that the word of God may not be reviled. First Timothy grounds slaves honoring masters in so that the name of God and the teaching may not be blasphemed. First Peter grounds wives' submission in so that they may be won without a word. First Timothy tells younger widows to marry and manage households so as to give the adversary no occasion for slander.

Where a command's own stated reason is missionary credibility inside a particular social order, and that order has been replaced by one where the identical behavior produces the opposite reputational effect, the letter and the purpose have come apart. What follows from that is arguable. That it happened is just reading the verse.

It depends on a dead institution. Temple sacrifice. Roman household law. The legal non-personhood of slaves. The total economic dependence of women.

It regulates an evil rather than instituting a good. Sometimes Scripture limits and makes a harmful practice less cruel without creating or praising it. Examples include divorce certificates, slave codes, and rules for captives. In such cases, it makes sense to read the law as an adjustment to human failure. Matthew 19 provides the biblical basis for this approach.

The author flags it himself. I say this as a concession, not a command. I have no command of the Lord, but I give my judgment. In view of the present distress. Paul is not shy about this and readers routinely ignore him.

Scripture contradicts or transcends it elsewhere. Women prophesying in the same letter that restricts their speech. Onesimus received as a brother in the same corpus that tells slaves to obey. Counter-instances don't automatically win, but a reading that can't accommodate them is running a debt it hasn't paid.

It's the symbol rather than the thing symbolized. The kiss, the basin, the veil. Where a sign no longer signifies what it signified, reproducing the sign doesn't reproduce the obedience.

Arguments that are weaker than they look

I want to be blunt here, because these get deployed constantly by people who should know better, and they're deployed by everybody.

It was countercultural, so it's timeless. Countercultural status shows the instruction wasn't absorption. It doesn't show it was permanent. Something can cut against the grain in service of a temporary goal.

It matches the surrounding culture, so it's disposable. Overlap might mean accommodation. It might also mean the surrounding culture happened to be right about something. Greco-Roman moralists condemned greed; the New Testament agreeing with them doesn't make greed situational.

The church has always taught this. Historical consensus is genuine evidence — the accumulated judgment of the community across many cultures — and waving it away is arrogance. But the church also reached near-consensus on the permissibility of slavery, on hereditary serfdom, on coercion in matters of faith, and on usury in the opposite direction, and then reversed. Evidence, not proof.

Modern scholarship has shown. Sometimes it has. Work on the authentein lexicon, on Artemis at Ephesus, on Roman household law, on ancient conceptions of desire has genuinely changed what a responsible reader can say. But this appeal usually hides the fact that the scholarship is split and the speaker has picked a side. The honest version names the dissent.

Loving people requires reading it this way. Not an argument until you've shown the reading is true, since otherwise it assumes what has to be proved. The mirror image is just as bad: treating a costly conclusion as self-authenticating because it's costly. Difficulty is not evidence of truth. Neither is comfort evidence of falsehood.

The plain meaning is obvious. The plain meaning was obvious to the pro-slavery exegetes, and they were more consistent readers of the surface than their opponents. Obviousness is a feeling, not a finding.

The point of all this

The criteria aren't a scoring rubric. Their use is in watching whether they converge.

Where they converge — greed, care for the poor, honesty, adultery — there's no interpretive problem and no debate happens. Where they diverge, divergence is the finding, and the right response is calibrated confidence instead of certainty. A method that returns high confidence on every question isn't a method. It's a machine for producing the answers its operator already had.

Marriage

What the world looked like

Marriage was a family transaction. Arranged between households, negotiated with dowry, aimed at property and alliance and legitimate heirs. Romantic attachment wasn't the basis of the match and wasn't expected to result from it, though people were pleased when it did. Roman girls were often betrothed in early adolescence, and the age gap between spouses could be enormous.

The law treated husbands and wives differently, as did the wider culture. Under the lex Julia de adulteriis of 18 BC, a wife's adultery was a crime. A husband could even be charged if he failed to divorce an adulterous wife. By contrast, a husband's sexual use of slaves or prostitutes did not count as adultery because the law defined the offense by the woman's social status. Jewish practice was stricter but still unequal. The husband held the right to divorce. The Mishnah states the difference clearly: a woman could be divorced against her will, while a man divorced by his own choice.

The question put to Jesus about divorce was technical. “Is it lawful to divorce for any cause?” was shorthand for a dispute between two Jewish schools. Hillel read Deuteronomy 24 as giving two grounds for divorce and allowed “any-cause” divorce. Shammai read it as one ground and limited divorce to sexual immorality. David Instone-Brewer argues that Jesus was asked to judge this dispute. If so, the exception clause rejects a recent legal change rather than giving a complete theory of divorce. I find this reading persuasive but not certain. It depends on rabbinic material whose date is unclear, and Jesus’ appeal to creation seems broader than the school debate alone.

Ancient households were religious units as well as families. The head of the household usually chose the religion for everyone. That is why a wife who converted on her own—the situation in First Peter 3—could cause a social crisis. Her choice was not treated as a private matter of conscience.

What the New Testament did to that

Some of this is startling and gets read past because it's familiar.

Symmetrical sexual obligation. 1 Corinthians 7:3–5 has no parallel in the Greco-Roman moralists. The husband should give his wife her conjugal rights, and likewise the wife to her husband; the wife doesn't have authority over her own body, the husband does; likewise the husband doesn't have authority over his own body, the wife does. Paul states the wife's claim first. He states both sides in identical grammar. He uses exousia reciprocally. In a culture organized around a man's sexual latitude, that is a demolition.

One standard of fidelity. The porneia prohibitions land on husbands exactly as on wives, criminalizing behavior Roman custom found unremarkable in a man. The vice list in 1 Corinthians 6 is addressed to men who had evidently been doing these things and thought nothing of it.

Permanence grounded in creation rather than statute. Jesus doesn't appeal to the divorce law. He appeals behind it.

Singleness as honorable. This is easy to miss and was genuinely radical. Augustus had legislated financial penalties for the unmarried and childless. Both Jesus and Paul commend voluntary celibacy as a gift and a strategic freedom. Which means the New Testament does not treat marriage as the default fulfillment of a human life, and any account of "the biblical view of marriage" that does has wandered off from its source.

Duty loaded onto the powerful party. Whatever you conclude about the structure, look at what husbands are actually commanded: not to rule, but to love as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, and not to be harsh, and to show honor on pain of hindered prayers. The measure of headship is set at self-donation to the point of death. That is not a comfortable command.

Where everybody agrees

This gets lost under the noise, so I'll list it. Across complementarian, egalitarian, Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline readings: marriage is covenant rather than contract. Sexual exclusivity binds both spouses by one standard. The union is comprehensive rather than merely physical. Sex belongs inside it. Conjugal obligation is mutual and symmetrical. The husband's duty is self-giving love measured christologically. Divorce is a grave evil and never a neutral option. Singleness is honorable. Children are a good and parents are constrained in how they treat them. And neither spouse's body is their own property, which has obvious implications for coercion inside a marriage that the tradition has been far too slow to draw.

That's a lot of agreement for an issue people treat as a battlefield.

Where they don't

Headship. The complementarian reading takes Ephesians 5 as establishing an asymmetric structure grounded in the Christ-church analogy rather than in social convention. The strongest form of the argument is this: Paul's warrant here is theological, not prudential. He doesn't say submit so the neighbors won't talk. He says it because of Christ and the church, and typology doesn't fluctuate with social conditions. Abolish headship and you don't liberate the analogy, you break it.

The egalitarian reading treats Ephesians 5:21 as the frame for what follows: “submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ.” It also notes that, in the best manuscripts, verse 22 has no verb of its own. It borrows the participle from the earlier line. On this view, the household code borrows a pagan form and then changes it. Egalitarians also argue that kephalē can mean something like “source” or “origin” in several passages from Paul. Payne and Westfall defend this claim at length, while Grudem strongly disputes it. Related household codes elsewhere also give reasons based on public reputation.

Two unsettled issues decide this question. The first is the meaning of kephalē. Neither side can close the case by choosing its preferred dictionary entry. Paul uses the word in at least two different ways, so context matters more than the dictionary alone. The second issue is what the comparison with Christ requires. Does it set a fixed social structure, or does it guide how one person should act within a structure that may change? Christ’s relation to the church is unequal in role. The open question is whether that comparison also requires unequal decision-making. Repeating the comparison more loudly does not answer it.

Here's what I think matters more than the verdict. Look at where the two readings land in practice. Both produce a husband commanded to self-donation measured by the cross, told not to be harsh, required to honor his wife on pain of hindered prayers, and denied ownership of his own body. The distance between a well-practiced complementarian marriage and a well-practiced egalitarian one is much smaller than the polemics need it to be. People have built identities on a gap that mostly isn't there.

Divorce and remarriage. There are roughly three positions. The first holds that the marriage bond cannot be dissolved. Supporters note that Mark and Luke give no exception. They read Matthew's exception as referring either to unions between close relatives or to sexual betrayal during betrothal. The second view says that adultery and abandonment by an unbelieving spouse end the bond. This is the Westminster position and the view held by most evangelicals. A broader covenant view follows Instone-Brewer's reading of Exodus 21. It argues that first-century marriage contracts recognized failure to provide food, clothing, and marital rights as grounds for divorce. Jesus did not cancel those grounds by remaining silent because he was answering a different question.

What's solid: Jesus tightened rather than loosened. Divorce is always a failure. And the New Testament nowhere requires anyone to stay in physical danger. What isn't solid is the complete list of grounds. Most pastoral traditions have concluded abuse qualifies, either under the 1 Corinthians 7 principle or the Exodus 21 tradition, and I think that conclusion is much stronger than the argument from silence against it — but it's an argument, and people should say so rather than citing a verse that isn't there.

What changed

Marriage is now chosen rather than arranged. Spouses are roughly matched in age and education, and wives often out-earn husbands. Women have independent legal personality, property, employment, and exit. Adultery expectations are symmetric in law and custom. Divorce is no-fault and more often initiated by wives. Contraception decoupled sex from childbearing. Life expectancy turned till death from a shorter promise into a sixty-year one. The household stopped being a productive unit. And religious affiliation became individual rather than corporate.

The last example matters most for this method. In the first century, a wife’s submission looked to outsiders like normal decency. Today, the same behavior may look like coercion to some people and like faithful resistance to modern culture to others. The result depends on the audience. Some passages give the reason “so the word of God may not be reviled.” If the public effect has reversed, the command’s wording and its purpose may now point in different directions. Complementarians fairly answer that Ephesians 5 gives no reason based on reputation. They argue that those other passages should not control the passage with the strongest theological basis. That reply has force. Still, the New Testament places both kinds of reasons beside closely related commands.

Women

Three questions get mashed into one here, constantly, by everybody. Separating them is most of the work, because they don't come out the same and pretending they do is how the argument stays stupid.

What the world looked like

Female literacy was low and formal Torah education for women wasn't normal. Which is why let a woman learn in 1 Timothy 2:11 reads, in context, as a positive command rather than a restriction — the striking thing is that women are to be taught at all. Both sides accept this. They differ on how much weight the next clause should carry.

Bruce Winter documents a first-century trend among elite women who used dress and public behavior to challenge the usual standards for married women. He argues that several New Testament teachings on women’s clothing respond to this trend. Scholars often cite his work, and the evidence gives it some support. Yet most of that evidence comes from elite and literary sources. Applying it to an ordinary church in Corinth or Ephesus therefore requires some inference.

An older theory about Ephesus has not held up. The Kroegers argued that 1 Timothy 2 responds to an early Gnostic form of Artemis worship that taught woman created man. Specialists on both sides have rejected this view because it relies on late evidence used out of context. Sandra Glahn’s recent work also challenges the common claim that Artemis of Ephesus was a fertility goddess. That removes a popular egalitarian argument. S. M. Baugh uses inscriptions to argue that Ephesus was not an unusual female-led society. In short, the specific Artemis-heresy theory is no longer credible. However, the letter itself strongly supports the broader point that 1 Timothy addresses false teaching in which some women were involved.

And prophecy ranked high. Paul lists prophets second after apostles and ranks prophecy above tongues precisely because it builds up the church. That women prophesied, with apostolic approval and regulation, is not in dispute by anyone. Any account of women's speech in the assembly has to fit that in somewhere.

Women teaching women

Start here, because it's barely contested and starting here changes how the rest reads.

Titus 2 doesn't permit older women to teach. It commands it. They're kalodidaskaloi — a compound built on the ordinary teaching root, applied to women without qualification — and they're to train the younger women. That's a mandate, sitting in the same corpus as the restriction, which tells hard against reading the restriction as a ban on women teaching as such.

It's modeled too. Priscilla explains the way of God more accurately to Apollos. Lois and Eunice get credit for Timothy's formation in the faith and the Scriptures. Women prophesy in the assembly under apostolic regulation.

And it's universally affirmed. Every complementarian statement affirms it. The Danvers Statement affirms it. No live tradition forbids it. What's contested is only where the boundary sits once you add mixed audiences, published books, seminary classrooms, and conferences — and those are line-drawing disputes inside a shared agreement, not disagreements about the thing itself.

Women in leadership

At its strongest, the complementarian case is not about silencing women. It limits one office—the role of elder with authority to teach and govern—to qualified men. The case begins with 1 Timothy 2:12. The verse forbids a woman to teach or authentein over a man and grounds the rule in creation order rather than local events. Supporters argue that a purely local problem would not need an appeal to creation. The discussion leads directly into the male terms used for overseers in chapter 3, which suggests a principle followed by its application. Similar patterns appear in three letters to two churches, and Paul says they reflect the practice of all the churches. The Twelve were also men. Jesus chose them from a larger group that clearly included women, even though he often challenged cultural limits on women. On this reading, the counterexamples do not prove the disputed point. Deborah led during a period described as national failure. Huldah gave an oracle but did not hold the office. Priscilla taught privately with her husband, and Phoebe served as a minister and patron.

I want to note a debate within complementarian circles. An older comparison appealed to the Trinity: the Son is equal in nature but under the Father in role, so different roles do not imply different worth. Beginning around 2016, complementarian scholars such as Trueman and Goligher strongly attacked this view. They argued that eternal functional subordination conflicts with the Nicene doctrine of the Trinity. Kevin Giles had made a similar case from outside the movement for years. Many complementarians now make their argument without this comparison, and I think that is the right choice.

The strongest egalitarian case says the counterexamples are not rare; they appear throughout Scripture. Deborah judged Israel, and Barak refused to go without her. Huldah confirmed Scripture for the king while Jeremiah was alive and available. Romans describes Junia as outstanding among the apostles. Every early church commentator who discusses the verse reads her as a female apostle. Chrysostom even praises the wisdom that made this woman worthy of the title. The alternative translation is modern, and scholars have challenged its language evidence. Phoebe is a diakonos of a named church, the same word Paul uses for himself and Apollos. She is also a prostatis, a word related to patron, protector, or leader. Women hosted house churches, which usually meant leading them in that culture. Paul also uses kopiaō, his word for ministry work, for Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa, and Persis.

And the restrictive texts have local triggers. 1 Timothy exists to counter false teaching at Ephesus — the letter says so in its third verse — in a crisis where women are named as vectors. The primary imperative of the passage is let a woman learn. A prohibition attached to an educational deficit most naturally addresses the deficit.

Then consider the key word. Authentein appears only once in the New Testament. Paul’s usual words for proper authority are exousia and proistēmi, but he uses neither here. Earlier examples of the authentein word group are rare and have several meanings. They range from murder and domination to, later, ordinary authority. Egalitarians say the negative meaning fits a letter that opposes abusive teaching. Complementarians, following Wolters, argue that the neutral meaning has good evidence and fits the passage better. Köstenberger adds a grammar argument: oude joins two actions with the same moral value, so if teaching is neutral, authentein must be neutral too. Critics challenge both his examples and his conclusion. Payne, Westfall, and Bartlett note that the Pastoral Letters often describe teaching as harmful. “Teach falsely and domineer” would therefore make good sense.

Honest summary: the database is small, the evidence permits both senses, and neither side gets to say the lexicon settled it.

And 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 is a mess. The verses appear after verse 40 in the entire Western tradition — D, F, G, the Old Latin — which is a hard fact demanding explanation. Payne argues the distigme-obelos marks in Vaticanus show its scribe knew manuscripts without them. Fee concluded in his commentary that they're a non-Pauline interpolation. Internally, an absolute prohibition on women speaking contradicts chapter 11 of the same letter, and the appeal to "the Law" without a citation is unlike Paul. People who keep the verses as Pauline mostly read them as addressing a specific kind of speech — disruptive questioning, or weighing prophecies — which is already a large concession away from the plain sense.

Women teaching men

This is the narrowest question and the sharpest, because it rests on one sentence, and I want to show how much of that sentence is up for grabs.

Gynaiki de didaskein ouk epitrepō oude authentein andros, all' einai en hēsychia.

Every substantive word is contested. Whether gynaiki and andros mean woman and man or wife and husband — the same nouns do both, First Peter uses them maritally, and the surrounding verses on adornment and childbearing give the marital reading a foothold. Whether didaskein means teaching generally or authoritative doctrinal instruction in the assembly. Whether hēsychia is silence or settledness, given that the identical word appears nine verses earlier describing the quiet life every Christian is supposed to lead. And authentein, discussed above.

I'll note one argument I think is weak, made by my own side of the aisle on this point: the claim that epitrepō being present indicative means I am not currently permitting, a situational ruling rather than a standing one. Paul uses first-person present for binding instruction elsewhere. Nothing much should be built on the tense either way, and egalitarians who lean on it are borrowing trouble.

Then the reason he gives for it. Adam formed first, then Eve; Adam wasn't deceived, the woman was. The complementarian reading takes primogeniture seriously as a real principle in that world, which it was. But verse 14 is a problem and careful complementarians know it. If Eve's deception disqualifies women from teaching, the argument proves all women are more deceivable — a claim almost no complementarian will defend out loud, and one that would equally disqualify women from teaching women and children, which nobody holds. The available answer, that Paul means something narrower about the reversal of order in the fall narrative, works. It also takes visible effort, which is itself information.

And verse 15 says she will be saved through childbearing, and nobody knows what that means. Preserved through childbirth. Saved through the childbearing, meaning the incarnation. Saved while fulfilling a domestic vocation. Spiritual well-being. A corrective to ascetics who forbade marriage. Five serious proposals, no consensus. A passage whose concluding clause defeats every commentator who's ever touched it is a shaky foundation for a confident universal rule, and that point gets made by egalitarians and conceded by the better complementarians.

Now the argument that actually moves me, and it isn't any of the above.

The head covering. Here's the thing I told you to hold onto. 1 Corinthians 11 stands the veil on creation, on the angels, on nature, and on the universal practice of the churches — four separate supports, every one of them the heaviest kind available. That is more ground, and better ground, than 1 Timothy 2 puts under the teaching restriction. And essentially nobody on either side treats the veil as binding.

The standard reply is that the substance transfers while the symbol doesn't — a visible marker of a real distinction survives, the specific fabric doesn't. That answer is coherent. I mean that seriously; it's not a dodge. But look at what it concedes. It concedes that a creation-grounded command can be culturally packaged. Once you've said that in chapter 11, you cannot turn around and treat creation-grounding as automatically decisive in the next book over. You've already admitted the principle. The argument now has to be about this text specifically, on other grounds, and most popular complementarian writing never does that work.

Where this lands

The tests point in different directions. I think that difference is the result, not a failure to reach one. Several facts support a permanent rule: the passages appeal to creation, repeat across several letters, and claim to reflect the practice of all churches. Other facts support a local rule. The letter opens with a specific false teaching. Nearby commands appeal to public reputation. Scripture gives many counterexamples. A rare word carries much of the argument. The wording of 1 Corinthians 14 is disputed, and Paul allows an exception involving head coverings.

Anyone reporting high confidence here is reporting something other than the state of the evidence. What I will say with confidence is that the defensible range is much narrower than the shouting suggests, in both directions. You cannot get to women may not minister publicly from these texts — the prophesying, the diakonos, the house churches, the coworkers, and Junia are all sitting right there. And you cannot get to 1 Timothy 2:12 isn't really there, because it is, and its warrant is creational, and waving at Ephesus doesn't dissolve it.

Both of those overreaches are extremely common. That's the actual scandal, not the disagreement.

Slavery

This section is the keystone of the argument. I placed it here on purpose. Everything said so far depends on it, and everything that follows will be measured against it.

Slavery is the one issue where essentially every Christian alive has concluded that the church's settled position goes past what the New Testament explicitly says — and concluded it as obedience to Scripture, not in defiance of it. Any method that can't explain that outcome is broken. Any method that explains it too easily has proved too much.

What the world looked like

Accuracy matters because two opposite mistakes can distort the case. One version cleans up the institution, while the other treats all forms of slavery as identical. People often choose whichever mistake helps their argument.

Slavery was everywhere in the Empire. Estimates for Italy run to a large minority of the population. It was assumed rather than debated, and there was no organized abolitionist movement in antiquity of any kind — not pagan, not Jewish, not Christian.

It wasn't racial. Slaves came from war, piracy, exposure of infants, debt, birth, and sale, and could be Greek, Syrian, German, Judean, or Italian. It was occupationally diverse: slaves worked mines and estates under conditions that killed them, and also served as physicians, teachers, accountants, business agents, and imperial administrators. Manumission was common enough that freedmen formed a recognized class, and Rome had to pass laws restraining the rate of manumission.

These qualifications should not soften the reality. Glancy and Harrill show what the laws and literature plainly say. Roman law treated a slave as property, not as a person. Slaves had no legal marriage, parental rights, or right to sue. Owners were generally allowed sexual access to enslaved people, and society expected it. Kyle Harper shows that Roman sexual ethics depended on the availability of enslaved bodies. This gives the New Testament's porneia commands a part that modern readers often miss: a Christian slave in a pagan home had no legal right to refuse sex. Courts accepted a slave's testimony only after torture. Crucifixion was a common punishment for slaves. Under the senatus consultum Silanianum, authorities could execute every slave in a household if the master was murdered. Officials enforced this law in AD 61 after the death of Pedanius Secundus, killing about four hundred people, including children, despite public protest.

So: not Atlantic chattel slavery, and a system of total domination that was routinely murderous. Both are true. Anyone using the first half to soften the second isn't doing history.

Ancient writers did criticize slavery, but their criticism changed little. Seneca urged owners to treat slaves humanely and recognized their shared humanity, yet he accepted the institution. Ilaria Ramelli traces a line of philosophical and religious criticism that reaches Gregory of Nyssa’s fourth-century sermons on Ecclesiastes. These sermons may contain the first complete ancient rejection of slavery as an institution. Gregory argues that owning a human being claims property that belongs only to God. He is the exception that proves the rule. It also matters that this argument finally appears in a Christian bishop who reasons from the image of God.

What the New Testament does and doesn't do

Doesn't. The New Testament nowhere condemns slavery as an institution, nowhere commands manumission, nowhere tells Christians to work for abolition. Say it plainly, because the alternative — pretending abolition sits on the surface of the text — is exactly the pretense that got destroyed in the nineteenth century.

Does. A set of moves, none of them abolition, all pointing one direction.

Slaves get addressed directly as moral agents, which the Greco-Roman household literature basically doesn't do. In Aristotle the head of house is the moral subject and everyone else is a management problem. To command a slave is to treat him as a person capable of obeying God, which is precisely what his legal status denied.

Masters get put under obligation and warned. Stop your threatening removes their characteristic instrument. There is no partiality with him puts master and slave under identical judgment, which is a direct strike at the status logic holding the whole system up.

Masters and slaves become brothers. 1 Timothy 6:2 addresses a situation Rome had no category for: a master who must regard his own property as a brother. Philemon pushes further — no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother, both in the flesh and in the Lord.

The trade gets condemned. Andrapodistai in the 1 Timothy vice list. And Revelation ends Babylon's luxury-goods inventory with slaves, that is, human souls, which is a deliberate and devastating placement.

Freedom is preferred where available. The syntax of 1 Corinthians 7:21 is genuinely ambiguous — mallon chrēsai has no expressed object, and "use freedom" and "use your present condition instead" are both grammatically possible. Most modern commentators favor taking the freedom, since mallon most naturally contrasts with the preceding don't be concerned. The minority reading has respectable defenders and was the patristic majority, Chrysostom included. I think "take your freedom" is more probable and I wouldn't stake much on it. But two verses later Paul says do not become slaves of men, and that is not the language of a man at peace with the institution.

Philemon, and what Paul couldn't say

John Barclay's reading of that letter is the most illuminating thing I've encountered on this whole subject.

Paul applies maximum pressure. I could command you but I'd rather appeal. Receive him as you would receive me. Charge any debt to my account. Also, you owe me your own self. I'm confident of your obedience, and I know you'll do even more than I say.

And he never writes the words free him.

Barclay argues that Paul is truly caught between theology and Roman law. The belief that Christians are siblings in Christ had not yet produced a settled view of legal slavery. Paul therefore pushes Philemon as far as a letter can while leaving the formal choice where Roman law placed it. On this reading, the letter’s famous ambiguity is not avoidance. It honestly records a conviction whose social results had not yet been worked out.

Which means the New Testament contains a principle its own authors didn't fully cash out. That is not a liberal claim. It's the most natural reading of the evidence on the one question where the modern church is unanimous.

The nineteenth century, which should frighten everyone

Here's the part I'd make every seminarian sit with.

In the American debate, the pro-slavery exegetes won the flat reading. Not by a hair. And the reading they won with was the one their opponents also claimed to want. They pointed out that the patriarchs held slaves without rebuke; that Mosaic law explicitly permitted slaveholding, including foreigners in perpetuity; that Jesus said nothing against an institution surrounding him on every side; that Paul sent a runaway back to his owner; and that Peter told slaves to submit to unjust masters specifically, not just kind ones. Their conclusion was that abolitionists had to explain away the plain sense and substitute feeling for exegesis.

Mark Noll documents the result. The method that American Protestants trusted most led to a terrible conclusion, and they had no shared method for correcting it. That is the “theological crisis” in his title. The dispute was finally settled by war rather than interpretation. This should humble anyone who treats careful biblical interpretation as an optional luxury.

Abolitionists had to build a different argument, mostly without the language later scholars would provide. They emphasized Exodus 21:16, which makes kidnapping a person for slavery a capital crime. The Atlantic slave trade violated that command on a massive scale. They also used Deuteronomy 23, which forbids returning an escaped slave to a master. The Fugitive Slave Act did exactly the opposite. They traced a movement through Exodus, Jubilee, the prophets, the image of God, Galatians 3:28, and Philemon. They argued that permission does not equal approval, the same move Jesus makes in Matthew 19. Finally, they noted that the racial claim supporting the whole American system had no biblical basis. Without that claim, the system could not have worked in law or in the economy.

Black interpreters had been reading Paul as an emancipationist for two centuries against the dominant white reading. Lisa Bowens documents it. They were dismissed at the time for reading with their interests rather than with the text. They were right, and the people dismissing them were the ones reading with their interests.

Three lessons, and they discipline everything else in this essay.

The plain sense can be catastrophically wrong. Not incomplete. Wrong, in a way that underwrote atrocity for centuries. Any hermeneutic that gives automatic priority to surface reading has to answer for 1860.

The readings that felt most rigorous were the ones aligned with the readers' material interests, and the readers could not see it. Everyone in that fight believed they were following the text. If your exegesis has never once cost you anything, that is information about your exegesis and not about the text.

The correction came from the excluded. The interpreters furthest from the dominant position saw what the dominant readers couldn't. Reading outside your tradition isn't a courtesy you extend. It's how you find out what you're structurally unable to notice.

Running the criteria

Here's what makes slavery the calibration case: everything converges.

Regulation of an existing evil rather than institution of a good. Stated purposes that are explicitly reputational — so the name of God may not be blasphemed, so that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior. Seed ideas that undermine the practice: Galatians 3:28, Philemon 16, the image of God, no partiality. Preliminary movement, since the instructions to masters have no real pagan parallel. The closely related practice condemned outright. No basis in creation, because slavery appears only in the wrecked world. An institution tied to a legal order that's gone. And the Old Testament's own arc running toward release — Exodus, sabbatical year, Jubilee, the prophets on oppression.

Every criterion points the same way. That's convergence, and it's why abolition isn't the church sheepishly departing from Scripture. It's the method delivering a result.

Now

Legal slavery based on ownership has now been abolished in every country. Christians played a major role in that achievement, including Quakers, the Clapham Sect, Black churches, and abolitionist clergy. They succeeded despite opposition from other Christians who could quote more verses.

The underlying thing didn't disappear. It went illegal and out of sight. Trafficking. Debt bondage, which is the largest category worldwide and works by manufacturing debts that can never be cleared. Forced labor in supply chains — minerals, fishing, agriculture, garments, construction — embedded in goods sold in every developed market. Domestic servitude tied to visa sponsorship arrangements that make leaving legally impossible. Forced and child marriage. Coercive prison labor where the legal exceptions are broad enough to drive through. The joint ILO, Walk Free, and IOM estimates put the global figure in the tens of millions.

The modern application here is direct, so I will be blunt. First Timothy 1:10 and Revelation 18:13 plainly condemn human trafficking and forced labor. No complex method is needed. The commands to masters also apply to every relationship with unequal economic power. These commands forbid threats, require justice and fairness, and remind the powerful that God judges without favoritism. Today, such relationships include employment, contracts, and the supply chains behind ordinary purchases. Christians who condemn trafficking in theory but never ask about the labor behind their own purchases have not applied these texts. They have only quoted them.

Sex

Two things before I start, and both are load-bearing.

This one isn't abstract for the people it's about. Whatever anyone concludes, the conclusion has consequences for real people — their families, their vocations, their standing in their churches, in some places their physical safety. That doesn't settle the exegesis and a document that let it would be worthless. It does set a standard for how the argument gets conducted, and it makes carelessness a moral failure rather than just a sloppy one.

The question of interpretation and the question of authority are different. What do the passages mean in their original setting? What should the church do now? Separating these questions brings unusual clarity to the debate. Some highly skilled scholars who personally support affirming conclusions still say the New Testament condemns same-sex sexual acts. William Loader makes this especially clear, and Luke Timothy Johnson says it directly. They then argue on separate grounds that the church should change its teaching. At the same time, the traditional view does not depend on the claim that no ancient person had a lasting same-sex orientation. Its strongest defenders do not make that claim.

Almost all the public argument happens at the wrong level because of this confusion.

Clearing out the dead weight

Both sides are carrying arguments that don't work, and I'd rather say so than be polite about it.

Sodom should be retired. Genesis 19 is attempted gang rape and a catastrophic violation of hospitality. Ezekiel names Sodom's guilt as pride, gluttony, prosperous ease, and neglect of the poor. Jude's sarkos heteras most plausibly points at the angelic identity of the visitors rather than the sex of anyone. Even Gagnon, who wrote the heaviest traditional treatment in print, concedes the passage is indirect at best. Its continued rhetorical use is a liability to the position it's meant to support, and people keep using it because it's vivid rather than because it's relevant.

Jesus' silence is over-read the other way. He also isn't recorded on incest, bestiality, or domestic violence, and nobody infers permission. Arguments from silence are weak in both directions, which is a point that cuts against whoever is currently making one.

Leviticus does not provide a clean split between ritual purity and morality. Affirming readers note that to'evah also describes food and ritual matters. They also point out that Christians do not follow the surrounding Holiness Code. Both points are fair. Yet Leviticus 18 and 20 focus on sexual and related wrongs: incest, adultery, sex with animals, and child sacrifice. These chapters say God judged nearby nations for such practices. The New Testament repeats these sexual teachings while clearly dropping the food rules. The chapters do not divide into the two neat groups this argument requires.

The strongest historical claim does not fit the evidence. Some argue that ancient people had no concept of lasting same-sex attraction. Yet Aristophanes' speech in the Symposium tells an origin story about lifelong inborn attraction. Astrologers and medical writers such as Ptolemy, Vettius Valens, Firmicus Maternus, Soranus, and Caelius Aurelianus also describe such desire as natural from birth. Roman writers mention same-sex weddings, usually with contempt, but still show that the practice existed. Brooten gathered much of this evidence while writing from an affirming position. She concluded that Paul probably knew this idea and still condemned the behavior.

Most well-informed affirming scholars now make a narrower and stronger claim. They argue that the typical cases known to Paul involved adult relationships with boys, exploitation, or social status. An equal and committed partnership was not the usual model he had in mind. That claim is defensible. The open question is whether it limits a ban whose stated reason is the difference between male and female, rather than exploitation.

The vocabulary

Malakoi means soft, and its range runs from luxurious and self-indulgent through effeminate in presentation to the passive partner in male intercourse. It isn't a technical term. Translations that render it flatly as "homosexuals" are overreaching, and its sense here depends heavily on the word sitting next to it.

Arsenokoitai is the crux and I think it's fairly clear. It's almost certainly a coinage, with 1 Corinthians 6:9 as the earliest known occurrence. It joins arsēn and koitē, and it maps onto the Septuagint of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13 nearly letter for letter — meta arsenos ou koimēthēsē koitēn gynaikos. That correspondence is the strongest single argument that Paul or his Hellenistic-Jewish milieu built the word straight out of the Levitical prohibition, and I find it convincing.

Scholars take both Boswell's male-prostitute reading and Dale Martin's exploitation reading seriously. Still, both are weaker than the explanation based on the Septuagint. Boswell's wider theory has also lost support among specialists. It is true that a compound word does not always equal the sum of its parts. But that warning does not settle this case because a specific source passage uses the same words that form the compound.

Romans 1

This is where the weight sits and where the arguments are best on both sides.

On the traditional reading, Paul connects idolatry with disorder in human life. People exchange the Creator for created things. Same-sex relations then serve as the main example because they reverse the created pattern of male and female, just as idolatry reverses Creator and creation. The repeated word exchanged holds the argument together. Paul’s stated reason is not abuse, temple prostitution, or uncontrolled desire. It is physis, read in light of Genesis. Verse 26 also mentions women, which rules out an explanation limited to adult men and boys. Finally, “consumed with passion for one another” describes mutual desire, not one-sided abuse.

The affirming readings, in descending order of how much they move me:

The instability of nature. Paul uses para physin of God grafting branches into an olive tree. He appeals to physis for hair length in 1 Corinthians 11, and nobody treats that conclusion as binding. So the term can't carry all the weight the traditional reading puts on it. This is the strongest objection on the table and I don't think it's been fully answered — the standard reply, that the senses are distinguished contextually, is true but a little convenient.

Excess rather than orientation. Paul describes people who gave up natural relations — acting against their own constitution, driven by insatiable appetite — which doesn't describe someone exclusively oriented acting in accordance with theirs. Brownson develops this at length. The reply is that physis in a Jewish frame means the created order rather than individual constitution, and reading it as personal constitution imports a modern category into a first-century word. I think the reply is right.

The rhetorical trap. Romans 1 catalogues Gentile vice so a Jewish audience will nod along, precisely so 2:1 can spring it — therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. On this reading Paul is voicing a conventional critique to demolish self-righteousness, not issuing a freestanding ruling. Clever, and it fails, because the trap only works if the audience's judgments are correct. Paul's argument through chapters 2 and 3 needs the vices to be genuinely vicious.

Cultic context. The frame is idolatry so the behavior may be tied to pagan rites. Weak. Paul mentions no temples and no cult prostitution, and his causal claim is about idolatry in general.

The positions, honestly stated

Traditional. Same-sex sexual activity goes against the design established in creation. The biblical passages agree with one another, and their reason comes from creation rather than local culture. The shared teaching of Christian traditions until the late twentieth century adds historical support. See Gagnon, Hays in 1996, DeYoung, and Sprinkle.

Affirming on exegetical grounds. The texts address exploitation and excess, not covenanted partnership. Brownson, Vines, Martin.

Affirming on interpretive grounds while accepting the traditional reading of the passages. This position agrees that the texts condemn the acts they discuss, but says the church should still revise its teaching. Support may come from a redemptive movement in Scripture, from Acts 10–15 where the Spirit reaches excluded people before theology changes, or from other cases in which the church has changed its reading. Johnson, Rogers, and Karen Keen take forms of this view. Richard Hays now does as well. He wrote the most influential traditional treatment of his generation but reversed his position with his son in 2024. His new case does not mainly reinterpret Romans 1; it traces an expanding pattern of mercy across the Bible.

Traditional in conclusion, reformed in practice. This view keeps the historic sexual ethic but judges the church’s treatment of gay Christians as a serious failure that requires repentance. Failures include demanding a change in orientation, treating marriage as the normal path for every Christian adult, and failing to build friendships and shared households that make celibacy livable instead of merely required. Wesley Hill, Ed Shaw, and Nate Collins represent this approach. It receives little attention in public debate because it meets neither side’s political needs. Yet many gay Christians actually live within it.

And Webb. He runs the same criteria that give abolition on slavery and a substantially egalitarian result on women, and lands traditional here, because the criteria point the other way. That is the most methodologically interesting fact in the debate.

Running the criteria

They converge, and they converge on the traditional reading of what the texts say.

The New Testament sexual ethic ran hard against its own culture — on adultery, on the sexual use of slaves, on prostitution, on divorce, on this. That's the mirror image of the slavery texts, which largely tracked the surrounding order. There are no seed ideas undermining the prohibitions. No breakouts. No preliminary movement toward acceptance to extend. And no stated reputational or missionary warrant anywhere, which is a striking absence given that the slavery texts and several of the women texts hand you one directly.

The other side points to different evidence. Ancient cases did differ from the modern question, and Paul does not always use physis in the same way. The church has also changed its reading before, especially on slavery and Gentile inclusion. In the Gentile case, the Spirit’s work appeared before the council formed its argument. Affirming readers also consider the visible results of traditional teaching in gay Christians’ lives. They cite well-documented harm from efforts to change sexual orientation, which every major professional group and many former supporters now reject. They apply Jesus’ principle that a tree is known by its fruit. Traditional readers answer that poor implementation caused the harm, not the ethic itself. The fourth position above tries to correct that failure. They also warn that a test based only on results could overturn almost any biblical command.

Here is my conclusion. Unlike the debate about women, most of the tests point in the same direction. Most critical scholars also agree about what the passages mean. This includes scholars who personally wish the texts said something else and admit that they cannot make that case. Their agreement is the best evidence that the conclusion does not simply come from traditional belief. They had strong reasons to reach a different result.

What the criteria don't settle is the authority question. Positions three and five above agree almost entirely about the Greek and disagree completely about what follows from it. That's where the disagreement actually lives, and forty years of verse-by-verse argument has never reached it, because it isn't a question about verses.

One final issue concerns integrity, not interpretation. Some churches treat same-sex relationships as grounds for breaking fellowship. The same churches may treat heterosexual divorce, remarriage, pornography, and sex before marriage as private pastoral concerns. People outside the church can easily see this double standard, and it seriously weakens the church’s credibility. This point is not an argument for an affirming position. Traditional writers such as Sprinkle and Hill make it strongly. It is damaging because it is true.

Three things fall out of that.

The method discriminates. It gives a confident abolitionist answer on slavery, a confident traditional answer on the sexuality texts, and an honest verdict of unresolved on the two gender questions. A method returning the same answer every time would be a preference wearing a lab coat. This one doesn't, which is the main reason I trust it.

The divergence on gender is the finding, not a failure. Appeals to creation against borrowed literary forms and reputational purposes. Restrictive texts against a pile of canonical counter-instances. The correct response to conflicting evidence is calibrated uncertainty, and the practical implication is that neither party should be treating these as boundary markers of orthodoxy. People have made careers out of certainty the evidence doesn't fund.

The slavery-to-sexuality analogy doesn't transfer cleanly, and this is the important one. It's the affirming side's most rhetorically powerful move — the church revised once against the plain sense, so it can revise again — and the table shows why it doesn't work at the level people usually deploy it. On slavery every criterion agreed. On same-sex practice most of them run the other way. The criteria are not doing the same thing in the two cases and saying otherwise is sleight of hand.

Where the analogy keeps real force is one level up. Not in the criteria, but in the bare precedent: the church did once revise against the apparent plain sense of its own texts, and in the Acts 15 case it did so following the Spirit's evident work rather than following a new exegesis. Whether that precedent extends here is a question about the nature of scriptural authority. It is not a question about Greek, it will never be settled by another article on arsenokoitai, and I don't think anybody should pretend it's been closed.

The test

Here's what I'd hold myself to and what I'd hold anyone else to.

If you accept the slavery argument and reject the sexuality one, or the reverse, name the criterion you're weighing differently and say why. Just name it. Preliminary movement, stated purpose, creational grounding, seed ideas, breakouts — pick the one that's doing the work and defend it.

If the honest answer is only that one conclusion feels comfortable and the other does not, then no method has been applied. A preference has been dressed up as a method. Pro-slavery interpreters did the same thing. They were more careful and more confident, yet completely wrong.

A method that hands you what you wanted every single time isn't a method. And a method that can never tell you no isn't worth having.
